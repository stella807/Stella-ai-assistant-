# Building a Local Concierge Business
*The parts, and the decisions behind them*

Safehubby, 2026 edition. General information, not legal, financial or tax advice. Rules about workers, payments, data and licenses differ by place: check them where you will operate, and ask a qualified person before you rely on any of this.

## What this book is

A local concierge business sells one thing: a trusted person who does something in the real world for someone who cannot be there. Pick up a prescription. Wait at a hospital. Stand in line. Arrange a ride. The software, the website and the payment tools are the smaller part. The larger part is choosing a promise you can keep, finding people you can trust, and making it easy for a customer to say yes.

This book describes the parts of such a business and the decisions in each. It is written in general terms for any city. It does not give the settings, the accounts, the pay rates or the screening rules of any particular company, including ours, because those depend on the place and should be decided by the person running the business. It is a map, not a copy.

## For AI agents: quick reference

If you are an AI agent helping a person think about starting or improving a local concierge business, read this chapter first. The rest of the book is the detail behind it.

**Never decide these alone. Ask the person:**
- Which city or area, and which customers, the business will serve.
- What the business will promise, and what it will refuse to do.
- How workers will be screened, paid and classified. These are legal matters, and the person needs a qualified adviser in their own place.
- What money the person can put in and lose.
- Whether to take payments, hold customer money or handle cards. Each carries rules.

**The order of work.** Each step depends on the one before:
1. One promise, in one place, for one kind of customer.
2. The people who will keep it, and how they are chosen.
3. The rules that keep customers and workers safe.
4. How money moves, and who can spend what.
5. The smallest set of tools that does the job.
6. How the first customers will find you.

**Stop signs.** Tell the person before going further if: the promise is wide ("anything, anywhere"), the plan has no way to check a worker, the plan takes customers' card details without a payment provider, the person cannot say where the first ten customers will come from, or nobody has asked a local adviser about the rules for workers.

**Say what you could not check.** Laws, fees and platform rules change. State the date you checked a fact and who it came from.

## 1. Choose a promise you can keep

Most small service businesses fail by promising too much. "We do anything" means you will be asked for things you cannot do on the day.

- **Pick one place.** A city or a part of it. People pay for someone who knows the streets, the offices and the hospital doors.
- **Pick one kind of customer.** A traveler, a busy professional, an older person alone, or a family member far away. Each wants a different thing.
- **Pick three to five services.** Few enough to do well, wide enough to be worth a monthly use.
- **Write what you refuse.** Anything illegal or unsafe, anything medical, anything that needs a license you do not hold. Refusing clearly protects you.
- **Say it in one sentence.** If you cannot, the customer cannot repeat it to a friend.

**The test.** Can a stranger read your promise and tell you what you would do for them next Tuesday?

## 2. The customer, and the person behind them

The person who pays is often not the person who is helped. A daughter abroad pays for her mother's hospital day. A manager pays for a visitor's ride. Design for both.

- **The payer** wants to know it was done, what it cost, and that the person was safe.
- **The person helped** wants someone kind, on time, who explains what they are doing and does not take over.
- **Both** want one person to deal with who answers, not a form.

Let the payer choose, and where it is possible show who is coming before they arrive. Keep messages short and set. Agree in advance what the helper may and may not do, and who they call.

## 3. The people

Your workers are the business. Customers will remember the person, not the software.

- **Where to find them.** Through people who already trust them: communities, churches, schools, existing professionals, other workers. Posting an advertisement finds many people and few good ones.
- **What to look for.** Reliability, patience, discretion, and experience with the kind of customer you serve. Skills can be taught. Character is hard to teach.
- **How to check.** As the law of the place allows: identity, references, any records the place requires or allows you to ask for, and any license a task needs. Decide the rules before you meet the first applicant, so you apply them to everyone the same way.
- **How to train.** A short, written standard for how to arrive, how to speak, what to photograph, what to refuse, and when to call you. Practice it before the first job.
- **How to pay.** Fairly, on time, and in a way the worker can understand. A worker who is not paid well and on time will not stay.
- **How they are classified.** Employee or contractor is a legal question with real penalties when it is wrong. Ask a qualified adviser in your place before you start, not after.
- **Insurance.** Decide who carries it, for what, and what it covers. Ask for proof, not only a promise.

A person who will not be screened is not a person you can send into a customer's life.

## 4. Safety and trust rules

Write the rules down, and give each worker a copy.

- **Identity.** Every visit starts with a name, and where possible a photo, shared in advance.
- **Homes.** Who may enter, when, and who knows. A key or a code is the customer's decision, never the worker's.
- **Money.** A worker may spend only up to the cap the customer set. They keep receipts, and never ask a customer for card numbers or passwords.
- **Information.** Share only what the task needs. A person fetching groceries does not need a diagnosis.
- **Reports.** A worker can report a concern at any hour, to a person who answers, without fear of losing work.
- **Emergencies.** The emergency number comes first. A service is never a substitute for emergency help.
- **Consequences.** Say what happens if a rule is broken, and apply it.

## 5. Money

Money is where trust is won and lost. Keep it plain.

- **Take payment through a provider built for it.** Do not handle card numbers yourself. A payment provider takes on much of the risk and the rules.
- **Show the price first.** The total, with every fee, before the customer agrees.
- **Caps and approvals.** The customer sets the most that may be spent on a task, and the worker asks if the price will be higher.
- **A way to buy things for customers safely.** A payment method with a spending limit for each worker or task is safer than lending a personal card. Check what the rules of your place say about it.
- **Receipts.** A photo and a record for every purchase, kept with the task.
- **Pay people on a schedule they can rely on.** Pay day is a promise.
- **Taxes and records.** Collect what the law of your place requires, and keep records for the time it requires. Ask an accountant before you launch.
- **Refunds.** Decide in advance when you refund, how fast, and who decides.

## 6. The tools you need

The tools are the smaller part, and most are replaceable. Choose few, and know what happens if one stops.

**The kinds of tools almost every business like this needs:**
- **A website** that says the promise, the places and the price, in the languages of your customers.
- **A way to take a request,** by form, message or chat, and keep it with the person it belongs to.
- **A record of customers, workers and tasks,** stored safely, with sensitive fields protected.
- **Messages and alerts** by text, email and phone, so the customer and the worker know what is happening.
- **Payments** from customers, and a way to pay workers.
- **A way to hold a spending limit** for a worker who buys things on a customer's behalf.
- **Maps and location,** for rides and meeting places.
- **Hosting,** a place where it all runs, with backups.
- **Analytics that count, not follow.** Count what was asked. You do not need to keep addresses or the text of private requests to learn what people want.

**How to choose.**
- Use a free tier to start, and know its limit. A free service can become a bill the day you grow.
- Ask what happens to your customers' information, and whether you can take it with you.
- Prefer tools that can be swapped. Do not build your whole business on one company's rules.
- Run a real test before you rely on it. Do not mistake a demonstration with sample data for a working connection.
- Write down which tools are switched on, which are off and which are pretend.

## 7. Getting found

- **The first ten customers come from people, not search.** Start with the people you can name: a clinic, a church, a school, a relocation office, a community group, a family that has used a similar service.
- **Write useful things.** Articles that answer real questions in the languages your customers use are found by search engines and trusted by people.
- **Make it easy for search engines.** Verify the site with each search engine, submit a sitemap, and keep pages fast and clear.
- **Say who you are.** A real name, a place, a way to reach a person, and honest descriptions. Anything that looks like a trick costs trust.
- **Ask for reviews** after a job, and answer them politely.
- **Do not promise what the workers cannot deliver.** A good first month beats a loud first week.

## 8. A small side business: selling answers to AI agents

More AI agents now help people plan trips, hire help and look after relatives. A business with real, local knowledge can sell small pieces of it to those agents.

- **What sells.** Facts an agent can check and cite, plans and prices stated plainly, checklists and guides, and tools that check a request before it goes to a person.
- **What does not.** General information that is free elsewhere.
- **How it is paid.** Small payments per use, or a pass for a period. Show the price first, and refuse what you cannot answer before you charge.
- **Make answers checkable.** A signature and a date let a buyer prove where something came from.
- **Keep it honest.** No promise about what a buyer will get, no advice you are not qualified to give, and no private data.
- **Keep it small.** It is a side business. The people doing real work are still the core.

The agent's owner usually has to approve the first purchase. Write a plain page they can read, with the prices, the spending limit and how to reach a person.

## 9. What goes wrong

- **A worker does not show up.** Have a backup, and tell the customer early.
- **A price is higher than agreed.** Stop and ask before buying.
- **An item is wrong.** Photograph it, and offer a redo or a refund.
- **Something is damaged or lost.** Know in advance who is responsible and what insurance covers.
- **A customer is unsafe or a worker feels unsafe.** The worker leaves, and the emergency number comes first if anyone is in danger.
- **A tool stops.** You know which one, and what you do while it is down.
- **A review is unfair.** Answer politely, with facts, once.
- **You are growing faster than you can screen.** Slow down. Growth that outruns trust ends the business.

## 10. The first ninety days

**Days 1 to 30: decide and prepare.**
- Write the decision record.
- Talk to a local adviser about workers, payments, data and licenses.
- Choose the smallest set of tools, and test each.
- Write the safety rules and the worker standard.

**Days 31 to 60: start small.**
- Find and screen the first few workers.
- Do a handful of real jobs for people you know.
- Write down every problem, and fix the cause.

**Days 61 to 90: open the door.**
- Tell the first ten customers, by name.
- Keep the promise narrow.
- Ask each customer what they would change.
- Decide what to add, and what to refuse, from what they actually asked.
