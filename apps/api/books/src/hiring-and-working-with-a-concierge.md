# Hiring and Working with a Concierge
*A buyer's guide to personal concierge, errand and assistant services*

Safehubby, 2026 edition. General information, not legal, financial or medical advice. Laws, prices and services differ by place and by company: check the details for where you live.

## What this guide is

Someone who runs your errands, waits with a parent, picks up a prescription, or arranges a ride can give you back hours of your week and a great deal of peace of mind. They can also be a source of frustration, or worse, when the arrangement is vague or the person is not who you thought.

This guide helps you decide what you actually need, compare what is on offer, check a service before you trust it, ask for things clearly, and handle it when something goes wrong. It is written for individuals and for families hiring for an older relative. It does not recommend any company, and it works the same whichever service you choose.

## 1. What a concierge is, and is not

The word is used for several different things. Knowing which one you want saves time.

- **A hotel concierge** helps guests of one hotel: restaurant bookings, tickets, directions. It is usually included in the stay.
- **A personal concierge or personal assistant** works for you: errands, bookings, shopping, waiting at home for a repair, managing small tasks. Priced by the hour, the task, or a monthly membership.
- **An errand or delivery service** does specific tasks, such as grocery runs or pickups. Often priced per errand plus the cost of the item.
- **Lifestyle or membership concierge** services bundle bookings and access for a monthly fee, often aimed at travel and events.
- **Companion or family-support services** accompany a person to appointments or wait with them in a hospital. They are about presence and practical help, not medical care.

What a concierge is **not:** a doctor, a nurse, a lawyer, an accountant or a financial adviser. A good concierge says so, and tells you when something is beyond what they can do. They also will not do anything illegal or unsafe, and you should be wary of anyone who offers to.

**Write down:** which of the five you want.

## 2. Work out what you need

Before you call anyone, take ten minutes to write down what you would hand off if you could. A simple way: keep a list for two weeks. Every time something takes your time and you wish someone else would do it, add it.

Then sort the list:

- **One-off tasks:** a pickup, a delivery, waiting for a technician.
- **Regular tasks:** a weekly grocery run, a monthly pharmacy pickup.
- **Urgent tasks:** things you need fast and cannot plan.
- **Sensitive tasks:** anything involving a house key, a door code, health information or money. These need extra care, and a longer chapter below.

Then think about how you would like it to work: a person with a car or without? A person who speaks a particular language? Someone who can come on short notice? A single person you get to know, or whoever is free?

Estimate hours. Most people overestimate what they will need at the start, and then use more once they trust the service. Start small.

**Write down:** your two-week list, sorted, and a rough number of hours a month.

## 3. How services charge, and how to compare them

Prices come in a few shapes, and the one that looks cheapest is not always the one that costs the least.

- **By the hour.** You pay for time, often with a minimum. Good for tasks that vary.
- **By the task.** A set fee for a set kind of job. Easy to understand; check what counts as one task.
- **Per errand plus the item.** A fee for the person's time and travel, plus the cost of what is bought, paid at cost. Check whether the fee changes with the size of the purchase.
- **Monthly membership.** A fee that includes some hours or tasks, with extras charged beyond that. Good if you will use it regularly; poor value if you will not.
- **Retainer.** A larger monthly sum that reserves a person's time for you. Only worth it if you really need that time held for you.

Watch for extra charges: travel or mileage, rush fees, minimum hours, cancellation fees, service fees, taxes, and a mark-up on items.

**How to compare.** Work out what you would actually pay for the same month of use at each service, not the headline price. Here is an *illustration*, with made-up numbers: if you need four errands and six hours of waiting in a month, a service that charges by the hour with no minimum, a service that charges by the task, and a membership with included hours can end up at very different totals. Write each one out and add it up.

**Write down:** the three prices for one imagined month.

## 4. Check the service before you trust it

Take time on this part. It is the work that prevents the problems in the rest of the guide.

**Ask these eleven questions,** and write down the answers:

1. What exactly is included, and what costs extra? Can I have it in writing?
2. How is the price set: by the hour, the task, or monthly? Are there minimums, taxes, travel or service fees?
3. Can I try a small first task before committing?
4. Can I cancel or change a booking, and from when does it cost money?
5. Is the person an employee or a contractor? Were they checked, and how?
6. Can I know who is coming before they arrive?
7. For rides: is the vehicle insured and the driver licensed?
8. What happens if the person is late, sick or does not come? Is there a backup?
9. Who do I call when something goes wrong, and how fast do they answer? A person, or only a form?
10. If you pay for things on my behalf, do I get receipts, and how do I pay you back?
11. What do you do with my address, health details and keys, and who can see them?

**Check the basics.** Is there a real business name, an address, a phone number that a person answers, and a way to reach someone? Does it have the registration or license that applies where you live? Does it carry insurance? Ask, and ask for the proof, not only the answer.

**Talk to a person.** A conversation reveals more than a web page. Notice how they answer: clearly, patiently, and without pressure.

**Ask for references,** or for how they handle problems. A service that cannot describe a time something went wrong and how it fixed it is a service to be careful with.

**Get the terms in writing.** At the least: what is included, the price, how and when you pay, how to cancel, what happens to your data, and who is responsible if something is damaged or lost.

**Write down:** the answers, the date, and the name of the person who gave them.

## 5. Trust and safety

You are letting a person act on your behalf and, sometimes, come near your home, your money and your family. A few habits protect you without making you suspicious.

**Identity.** Know who is coming. Ask for a name and, where it is offered, a photo, in advance. Check that the person who arrives matches. A reputable service will not be offended.

**First meetings.** Meet in a public or shared place the first time. Do not let anyone into a home until you know them, and share with someone you trust who is coming and when.

**Keys and codes.** Think carefully before sharing a key or a door code. If you must, use a temporary code, change it afterwards, and write down who has what.

**Money.**
- Give the person a **cap**: the most they may spend on a task.
- Prefer a **payment method made for the task** with a spending limit, over your own card details. If a service wants your full card number, your PIN, or your bank login, be careful.
- Get a **receipt** for every purchase, ideally a photo.
- Never pay a person you do not know in gift cards, cash apps to a personal account, or cryptocurrency. These are hard to recover.
- Do not pay large sums in advance.

**Privacy.** Share only what is needed. Do not send passwords. Ask how long your information is kept and how to have it deleted.

**Red flags.**
- Pressure to decide now.
- Prices that are vague or change.
- No business details, no written terms.
- A request for payment in a way that is hard to reverse.
- A request for your card numbers, PINs or passwords.
- A person who will not tell you their name.
- Anything that feels wrong. Trust that.

**For older adults.** Older people are often targeted by scams. If you are hiring for a parent, agree on how it will work: who is the contact, who sees the receipts, and how your parent can say "something does not feel right" and be heard.

## 6. Ask clearly

Most problems with errands come from a vague request. A good request answers these questions:

- **What** is needed? Be specific: brand, size, quantity, or "no substitutions."
- **When** is it needed by, and how firm is the time?
- **Where** is it going, and where should it be left?
- **How much** can be spent, at most?
- **What if** it is not available: substitute, call me, or skip it?
- **How will I know** it was done: a photo of the item, a receipt, a message?
- **Who** is the contact for questions, and by what means?

A template is in Appendix A. Here is an example: "Please pick up my prescription at the pharmacy on Main Street, which should be ready after 2 p.m. under the name Garcia. Bring it to the front desk at my mother's building before 5. Spend nothing beyond the copay of about $12. If they say it is not ready, call me before leaving. Send a photo of the bag and the receipt."

## 7. Building a good working relationship

- **Start with a small task,** as a trial. You learn how they communicate, how they handle problems, and whether you like working with them.
- **Say what went well and what did not.** Short, kind feedback works. A good person wants to know.
- **Decide how you will communicate:** text, calls, or a booking page. Say how fast you expect an answer, and be reasonable about how fast you give one.
- **Give lead time** for regular tasks, and say what counts as urgent.
- **Keep a short record** of tasks, costs and anything unusual.
- **Treat the person with respect.** They are a professional. The best arrangements are ones both sides are glad to continue.
- **Ask about tipping** or gratuity if you are unsure. Practices differ.

## 8. If you are hiring for an older parent

Hiring for someone else adds a layer. A few principles help.

- **Involve your parent.** It is their life. Ask what help they would welcome, and what they would not. Respect "no."
- **Introduce them first,** when you can, so the helper is not a stranger at the door.
- **Decide who the contact is.** One name for the service to call, so messages are not lost.
- **Share only what is needed.** A helper who picks up groceries does not need medical details.
- **Make routines.** The same person, the same day, the same way, helps people who are anxious.
- **Have a backup.** Who goes if the helper cannot come? Who is told?
- **For a hospital day,** a companion can wait with your parent, listen, take notes and bring things. They are not there to make medical decisions or to give medical advice. Agree in advance what they may and may not do, and who the hospital should call.
- **Keep an eye out,** gently. Look at receipts. Ask your parent how it went.

## 9. When something goes wrong

Sometimes a person is late, an item is wrong, a price is higher than agreed, or something is damaged. Stay calm and follow the steps below.

1. **Write down what happened,** with times and, if you can, photos.
2. **Contact the service** and say what went wrong and what you would like: a refund, a redo, an apology. Do it in writing, so there is a record.
3. **Give them a short, reasonable time** to answer.
4. **If it is not resolved,** ask for a manager or the person responsible, and repeat your request in writing.
5. **If you paid by card,** contact your card issuer promptly. There are time limits for disputes, so do not wait.
6. **If you suspect fraud,** stop paying, keep every message and report it to your local authorities and to your payment provider.
7. **If someone is in danger,** call the emergency number first. A service is never a substitute for emergency help.

This is general information, not legal advice. If a large sum or a serious harm is involved, talk to a qualified person where you live.

## 10. Ending the arrangement

- **Cancel in writing,** following the terms you agreed. Keep a copy.
- **Settle the account:** check the last invoice against your record of tasks.
- **Take back** keys, codes and any items, and change any codes you shared.
- **Ask for your information to be deleted,** if you wish, and keep the answer.
- **Leave honest feedback** if you can. It helps the next person.
