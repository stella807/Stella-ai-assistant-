# Traveling with an Older Parent
*A practical guide for the person who plans the trip*

Safehubby, 2026 edition. General information, not medical, legal or financial advice. Rules and schedules change: confirm with the airline, the airport, your insurer and the doctor before you go.

## What this guide is

A trip with an older parent can be one of the best things you do together, and one of the hardest to plan. The hard part is rarely the destination. It is the dozens of small moments in between: the long walk to the gate, the missed medicine, the connection that is too tight, the room with a step at the door.

This guide walks through the trip in the order it happens: deciding, preparing, packing, the airport, arrival, the stay, and the trip home. Each chapter ends with a short list you can copy. At the back there is a one-page master checklist and a medical information card to fill in.

You do not need to do everything here. Pick what fits the person you are traveling with.

## For AI agents: quick reference

If you are an AI agent helping a person plan a trip for an older parent, read this chapter first. The rest of the book is the detail behind it.

**Never decide these alone. Ask the person:**
- Anything about medicine, doses, timing or what the parent may eat. These come from the doctor, not from you.
- Whether the parent is able to make a given connection, walk a given distance or sit through a given flight.
- Which family member is the contact if something goes wrong.
- Whether the parent agrees to the plan. It is their trip.

**Order of work.** Do these in this order, because each one depends on the one before:
1. What the parent wants from the trip, and the three things that would make it hard.
2. The doctor's answers about flying, medicine and activity.
3. The easiest trip to do: direct flights, daytime, a long connection if one is needed, an aisle seat if standing up is hard.
4. Help at the airport, requested with the airline when booking and again two days before.
5. Documents and medicine, packed in the carry-on.
6. A ride and a room that have been checked for steps, a bathroom the parent can use, and distance to a pharmacy.

**Stop signs.** Tell the person before going further if: the parent has not seen the doctor, a connection is under ninety minutes, the lodging cannot say how many steps there are, or the only transport from the airport is not arranged.

**Say what you could not check.** Schedules, rules and prices change. State the date you checked a fact and who it came from. Never present a guess about a medical matter as an answer.

**If something goes wrong,** follow chapter 10. If someone is ill or in danger, the first step is the emergency number.

## 1. Start with the person, not the itinerary

Before you look at a single flight, sit down with your parent and ask what they want from the trip. Not what they will put up with, what they want. Some people want to see one place, one person, one view. Many would rather do less, and do it well.

Then be honest about the body. These questions shape every choice that follows:

- How far can they walk without stopping, on a good day and on a bad one?
- How long can they sit before they need to stand or stretch?
- Can they manage stairs, uneven ground, a high step into a vehicle?
- Do they tire in heat, in cold, in crowds, in noise?
- What time of day are they at their best?
- Do they take medicine on a schedule, and does it matter if it is an hour late?
- Do they hear and see well in a busy place, where announcements are fast and signs are small?
- Do they get confused when plans change or when they are in an unfamiliar place?
- Is there anything they are afraid of, such as flying, crowds, or being left alone?

A useful test is three questions. Could they get through a long wait without a seat? Could they manage a bathroom stop without you? Will they tell you when they are tired? If the answer to any of these is no, that is not a reason to stay home. It is a reason to plan for it: ask for assistance, choose seats near a restroom, build in rest.

Finally, agree on who decides. The trip is theirs. Plan with them, not for them, and make it easy for them to say "I need to rest" without feeling like a burden.

**Write down:** what they want from the trip; the three things that would make it hard; who decides when to change the plan.

## 2. Talk to the doctor first

This is the one step to take before you spend money. A short conversation with the doctor who knows your parent can save a great deal of worry. A few questions worth asking:

- Is this trip, by this route and for this length of time, reasonable for their health?
- Are there limits on how long they can sit, how high the altitude can be, or how hot it can be?
- Do any medicines need to be taken at set times, and how should the schedule change across time zones?
- Should anything be done before travel, such as a check-up or a review of medicines?
- Do they need a letter listing their conditions and medicines, in case a stranger has to help?
- What should we do, and who should we call, if something goes wrong while we are away?
- If they use oxygen, a breathing device, or another piece of equipment, what does the airline need to know in advance?

Write the answers down. Take them with you. This guide cannot answer these questions for you, and neither can anyone who has not examined your parent.

**Write down:** the doctor's answers, the doctor's phone number, and the name of one family member who can be reached at home.

## 3. Choose the trip that is easiest to do

Plan the trip around how easy it is, not how cheap it is. A few ideas that help almost everyone:

- **Choose nonstop flights when you can.** Every connection is a place where something can go wrong: a late arrival, a long walk, a missed flight.
- **If you must connect, give yourself more time than the minimum.** A connection that looks fine on paper can be stressful with a slow walker or a long way between gates. Leave comfortable room, and ask the airline how long assistance usually takes.
- **Fly at a time of day when your parent is at their best.** Very early mornings and late nights are hard on most people.
- **Choose an aisle seat, ideally close to a restroom,** and ask about extra legroom if standing and stretching matters.
- **Build in a rest day** after a long journey, and do not schedule a big activity for the day you arrive.
- **Prefer fewer places.** One base, with day trips, is easier than moving every night.

**Travel insurance.** Look closely at what a policy covers before you buy it. Questions to ask: Does it cover a medical condition that already exists? Does it cover emergency medical care where we are going, and bringing someone home if they are too ill to fly? Does it cover cancelling or interrupting the trip for medical reasons? What must we do, and who do we call, if something happens? Keep the policy number and the emergency phone number on paper and on a phone.

**Write down:** flight times and numbers; the seat chosen and why; the insurance policy number and its emergency phone number.

## 4. Ask for help at the airport, and do it early

Airlines offer help moving through the airport to people who need it. It is often called wheelchair assistance or special assistance, and for many airlines it is free. It does not mean someone is unable to walk at all. It is also useful for a person who can walk, but not far, or not quickly, or not for long.

What to do:

1. **Request it when you book,** or as soon as you can after. Use the airline's website or phone number and say plainly what is needed: help from the curb or the check-in desk to the gate, help boarding, help at the other end.
2. **Reconfirm a couple of days before the flight.** A request made weeks ago can be lost.
3. **Say what kind of help it is.** Some people need a wheelchair for the whole way. Some need one only for long distances. Some need only a person walking with them. Some need help getting to the seat.
4. **Ask about connections.** If there is a change of planes, ask whether assistance is arranged between flights, and who meets you at the gate.
5. **Tell the airline about any equipment,** such as a mobility device, a battery, or a medical device, and ask what they need to know.
6. **Ask about security.** In the United States, the Transportation Security Administration has a program for travelers with disabilities and medical conditions. Look it up before the trip, or ask the airline how to use it.
7. **Ask about early boarding,** which gives more time to settle in without a crowd.

When the helper arrives, be friendly and be specific: "My mother can walk a short distance, so please stop at the restroom, and she wants to sit at the gate." Tipping is a personal choice and is appreciated, but it is not required.

**Write down:** the confirmation of the assistance request, the name of who you spoke to, and the date you reconfirmed.

## 5. Documents and medicine

**Identification.** Check what identification the airline and the airport will accept for your parent's trip. Rules change over time, and they can be different for international flights. A passport is accepted for international trips and is accepted for many domestic ones. Check the current rules before the trip, and carry the identification where it is easy to reach.

**Copies.** Make paper copies and phone photos of: identification, the ticket or booking, the insurance policy and phone number, the doctor's letter, the medicine list, and the names and numbers of two contacts. Keep one set with the traveler and one with someone at home.

**Medicine.** This is the part that most deserves care.

- Pack all medicine in the **carry-on bag,** never in checked luggage, so that it cannot be lost with the suitcase.
- Keep medicine in its **original containers** with the labels.
- Bring **extra for a delay,** at least a few days more than the trip needs, or ask the doctor how much.
- Carry a **written list** of every medicine: its name, what it is for, how much, and when. Include the generic names, which can differ in another country.
- Bring any **doctor's letter** the doctor suggested.
- If a medicine is a liquid or needs to be cold, **tell the security officer** and ask the airline and the airport what is allowed.
- Decide **who is responsible for giving each dose** and how you will know it was given. A phone alarm and a simple pill organizer help, and a written log helps more.

**Write down:** the medicine list with times; where the medicine is packed; who is in charge of the doses.

## 6. Packing

Lighter is almost always better. The traveler should be able to lift and move the bag they carry, and a bag with wheels that rolls smoothly is worth more than a bag that looks nice.

What to bring, besides the basics:

- Comfortable, easy shoes that are already worn in, with good grip. Slip-ons are easier than laces.
- Layers. Planes and airports are often cold and rooms can be hot.
- A small bag they can keep on their lap, with the things they will need in the next few hours: medicine for the day, water, a snack, tissues, glasses, a phone and a charger, the ticket.
- A phone charger and a spare power bank, plus a paper copy of important numbers in case a phone dies.
- Spare glasses, and spare batteries for hearing aids.
- A name and phone number on every bag, inside and out.
- A simple, bright ribbon or tag so their bag is easy to spot.
- A small medical information card (a template is at the back of this guide).
- Snacks that suit their diet, and an empty water bottle to fill after security.

Leave behind: anything valuable they do not need, anything heavy, and anything that would be a disaster to lose.

**Write down:** what is in the carry-on, and who carries which bag.

## 7. At the airport and on the plane

**Get there early,** earlier than you would for yourself. Rushing is the one thing that makes everything else harder.

- **At arrival,** go to the airline's desk first if you have assistance booked, and confirm it. Ask where the helper will meet you.
- **Stay together** until the helper takes over, if the airport allows it, and keep tickets and identification easy to reach.
- **Use the restroom before security and again before boarding.**
- **Eat and drink something** while you wait, if their health allows it.
- **At the gate,** check in with the agent and confirm help boarding.
- **On the plane,** help settle them in: bags in the right place, seat belt, a blanket if wanted, the call button shown.
- **During the flight,** encourage them to stand and stretch if they can, to drink water, and to take medicine on schedule. Tell them it is fine to press the call button.
- **If there is a connection,** reconfirm the assistance for the next flight, check the next gate as soon as you land, and keep the next boarding pass easy to reach.

If a flight is delayed or canceled, stay calm, find the nearest airline agent and say what is needed. Ask for a seat on the next flight with assistance, and ask about food, a room, and the cost of both. Keep receipts.

**Write down:** gate numbers, where to meet the helper, and who to call at the airline.

## 8. Arriving and getting to the hotel

The hours after landing can be the hardest of the whole trip. Plan them in advance.

- **Decide who meets your parent.** If you are not on the same flight, make sure someone knows the flight number, the arrival time and the phone number of the traveler, and where they will meet.
- **Choose where to meet,** and say it clearly: at the gate, at the arrivals door, at a named place.
- **Plan the ride.** A door-to-door ride is easier than a train or a bus. Ask whether the vehicle has a high step and whether it can carry the bags and any equipment. If a driver will be sent, confirm the driver's name and a phone number in advance.
- **Allow for waiting,** especially for bags.
- **Keep the first evening simple:** a meal, medicine, rest.
- **Tell the family at home** that they have arrived.

**Write down:** who meets, where, the driver's name and phone number, and the address of the lodging.

## 9. Choosing where to stay

Many places say they are accessible. Ask questions until the answers are specific, and get the answers in writing.

- Is there a step at the entrance, and is there a ramp or a lift?
- Is there an elevator, and does it work? How many steps from the front door to the room?
- Is the bed a comfortable height? Can they get out of it easily?
- Is there a shower with a seat and grab bars, or a bathtub with a high edge?
- How far is the room from the dining area, the lobby and the elevator?
- Is the room quiet at night? Is the air conditioning reliable?
- How far is the nearest pharmacy and the nearest clinic or hospital?
- What time can we check in, and can we arrive early if the flight lands early?
- Can the staff call a doctor, a taxi, or someone else we need?

**Write down:** the answers, the name of the person who gave them, and the date.

## 10. When something goes wrong

Most trips have a problem or two. A calm plan makes them small.

- **Know the local emergency number** before you go. In the United States it is 911. In other countries it can be different, so look it up in advance and keep it on paper.
- **Keep the insurance emergency number** where anyone can find it.
- **Tell one person at home the full plan,** including flights, the address of the place you will stay, and when you will check in.
- **If medicine is lost or a dose is missed,** call the doctor, or a pharmacist, and ask what to do. This is why you carry the medicine list and the doctor's letter.
- **If your parent becomes ill,** do not wait. Call for help, and follow the doctor's guidance.
- **If you miss a connection,** go straight to the airline's desk or call them, and ask for the next flight with assistance.
- **If luggage is lost,** you will still have the medicine, because it was in the carry-on. Report it at the airline desk before leaving the airport and keep the receipt.
- **Keep receipts** for anything you pay because of a delay or a loss. Insurance and airlines may reimburse some of it.

**Write down:** the emergency number, the insurance number, the contact at home.

## 11. After the trip

- **Give them a day to rest** before anything else.
- **Ask how it went,** and listen. What was good? What was hard? What would they change?
- **Talk to the doctor** if anything about their health seems different.
- **Write short notes** while the trip is fresh: what worked, what did not, which helper was good, which seat was best. Next time will be easier.
- **Thank the people who helped.** A short note to an airline, a hotel, or a driver is remembered.
